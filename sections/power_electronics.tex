\section{Power Electronics System}
\label{sec:power}

To enable physically untethered operation, we used an iterative process to engineer a fully power autonomous robotic system with 3 main components: a flying robot, a custom lithium-ion battery, and a power-electronics circuit. In this process, we co-designed each system to maximize the total power-density of the robot. This thesis focuses on the power-electronics circuit to implement a sub-gram high-voltage power electronics circuit using off-the-shelf components and commercial flex PCB manufacturing process. In the process, we investigated different converter implementations and driving waveforms to minimize battery weight and maximize mechanical output. The success of this project will unshackle the robot from stationary power amplifiers, bringing soft-actuated flying robots closer to practical applications.

\subsection{Custom battery}
For powering the robot, we worked with another research lab to produce custom lithium ion batteries from dry cells. The battery electrolyze was formulated with high mobility for high power density in exchange for lower capacity. The cells were then encapsulated with minimal packaging materials, and appropriately sized electrodes were welded on. The power-density of these custom batteries was measured in the lab as the most important metric for designing with the circuits and the robot. The custom batteries achieved power density of more than $3 W / g$, 10 times higher than commercial lithium ion batteries (see figure \ref{fig:batt-density}).

\subsection{Waveform Optimization}
% As DEAs are capacitive loads, the electrical drivers need to cycle charges in and out of the actuators each actuation cycle. Therefore, the 
As part of weight-reduction strategies, the driving waveform was optimized to reduce peak power requirements such that the battery size can be minimized. When multiple driving DEAs, we can drive them out-of-phase such that one DEA will be charging while the another one is discharging to reduce the net current thus power from the battery. At the same time, the charging and discharging should be with constant power for minimal input current peak. Furthermore, the driving waveform was also optimized to produce high fundamental voltage with lower actual output voltage. This allowed the circuit to be implemented with smaller components, reducing the circuit's weight. The resulting ideal waveform would have each actuator channel driven with $\frac{\pi}{2}$ phase for reducing net current. It would also be a pseudo-square wave with approximately quadratic rise and fall time of 25\% of the period (see figure \ref{fig:waveform}).

\subsection{Driver Circuit}
On high-level, the power-electronics system needs to receive voltage commands and drive each actuator to the commanded voltage. This requires the system to have controllable output channels and wireless communication system for operation without physical tethers. We accomplished this with two power converter topologies and a 2-MCUs system with proprietary communication protocol.

\subsubsection{Topologies}
Two bidirectional circuit topologies were investigated for driving the DEAs: independent converters and shared converters. With independent converters, each channel has its own bidirectional flyback converter for charging and discharging (see figure \ref{fig:independent-converter}).

With shared converters, all 4 channels share a coupled-inductor boost converter for charging and a flyback buck converter for discharging. Each channel will then be consecutively connected to the appropriate converter using high-side switches (see figure \ref{fig:shared-converter}).

\subsubsection{Communication and Control}
A dual-core chip, CY8C6245 from Infineon, was used as the main controller for the circuit with a STM32WB05 wireless MCU for communication. In this setup, the CY8C6245 main CPU core was used to handle high-level communication and command. It communicates with the wireless MCU to receive voltage commands and forward the commands to the secondary core. The secondary core drives the converters with hardware PWM signals. It also senses the input battery voltage and the actuator output voltages to vary the PWM signals accordingly and stop charging when the actuators reached the commanded voltages.

\subsubsection{Lightweight PCB}
Each circuit topology was implemented on 3 by 3 centimeters double-sided flexible PCBs with 25 $\mu$m polyimide substrate and 1/3 oz copper (see figure \ref{fig:converter-pcbs}). For minimum weight, the PCB was produced with minimal to no solder masks and silkscreen. Wherever copper pours were used, the copper area would be hatched to reduce copper usage as well. With these weight reduction methods, the produced PCBs weighted around 100 mg for both systems.

\subsection{Preliminary Results}
These driver circuits have successfully driven capacitive loads ranging from 40 nF at 100 Hz to 100 nF at 50 Hz at up to 520 V. The output voltage can be commanded wirelessly with update rate of up to 400 Hz, allowing untethered control (see figure \ref{fig:controlled-waveform}). With the shared converters circuit, we demonstrated controlled take-off while the circuit was powered by a power supply off-board. The shared converter circuit had also been tested with the custom batteries, confirming that power autonomous operation is possible (see figure \ref{fig:battery-powered}). At the time of writing this proposal, we are working on scaling the robot and the battery to fit with the independent converters circuit and producing a new flex PCB to address thermal and parasitic capacitance issues.
